Growing and keeping a cut-foliage crop fresh for market

Dracaena reflexa ‘Song of India’ is sold as cut foliage, and growers lose value in two places: in the field and after harvest. My thesis set out to fix both with a spacing and fertiliser schedule and a complete post-harvest protocol.

  • DesignsFactorial RBD (field), CRD (post-harvest)
  • Soil analysisOrganic carbon (Walkley & Black), available N (alkaline permanganate), P (Bray-1), K (flame photometer), pH, EC, bulk density, porosity, water-holding capacity
  • Plant analysisN (Kjeldahl digestion & distillation), P (vanado-molybdo-phosphoric yellow colour), K (flame photometry), nutrient uptake, total chlorophyll

In the field

I compared two spacings (45 × 45 cm and 45 × 30 cm) against three nutrient schedules and an untreated control, then tracked growth, soil nutrient status, plant nutrient content, uptake and chlorophyll through the season. I handled the crop end to end: planting, nutrition, cultural operations, pest and disease watch, and harvest.

Result: 45 × 30 cm spacing with 48:6:12 g N:P₂O₅:K₂O per plant per year performed as well as the wider spacing, and fits 25% more plants on the same land. That is the recommendation the study makes to growers.

The post-harvest protocol I developed

  1. Harvest when the plant reaches about 3 feet.
  2. Condition shoots by removing dried and diseased parts. Skip precooling: it did not improve vase life.
  3. Pulse in 5% sucrose for four hours.
  4. Pack with cut ends plugged in moist cotton, shoots wrapped in polythene, inside corrugated fibre-board boxes with KMnO₄ sachets.
  5. Store up to six hours in an air-conditioned room at 20 °C.
  6. Hold in 2% sucrose at the point of sale. It performs close to distilled water, which most farmers cannot get.

The Pepper Canopy: a resort where the architecture grows a cash crop

An eco-luxury resort concept where funnel-shaped structures double as standards for black pepper. Guests walk under a living canopy, and the property earns from the harvest as well as the rooms.

  • Site fit2,000–3,000 mm rainfall, 18–29 °C, loamy lateritic soils: suitable if drainage is designed in
  • BenchmarkGardens by the Bay, Singapore, studied for how structure can serve both planting and visitors

What I planned

I wrote the crop side of the concept so that it could actually be built and farmed: how the structures must be surfaced for pepper to climb, how each vine is planted, fed, watered and protected, and how the risks to both crop and guests are handled.

VarietiesHigh yield (Panniyur series), disease tolerant (IISR Sakthi, Thevam), premium quality (Karimunda)
Climbing structureGalvanised steel frame with 5–7.5 cm mesh and 3–4 mm wire, roughened so aerial roots grip; coir or jute ropes to train young vines
PlantingTwo-node semi-hardwood rooted cuttings; 50 × 50 × 50 cm pits 15–20 cm from the frame, enriched with FYM, neem cake, ash and Trichoderma
WaterDrip with 2–3 emitters per vine at 2–4 LPH and fertigation; 8–10 L per vine every 5–7 days in the dry season; canopy runoff to rainwater tanks
NutritionOrganic base plus 50:50:150 g N:P₂O₅:K₂O per vine per year in two splits; zinc and molybdenum where deficient; lime in alternate years
ProtectionIPM for pollu beetle, scales and top shoot borer; drainage, Bordeaux mixture and biocontrols for foot rot; sprays timed outside guest hours

The business logic is simple: pepper adds heritage, a guest experience (trail walks, harvest workshops, spice-to-table dining, retail packs) and a second income line.
